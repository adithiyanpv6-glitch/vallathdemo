---
title: John Donne and Metaphysical Conceits
author: John Donne
period: Early Seventeenth Century
topic: Metaphysical Poetry
---

The Metaphysical poets, grouped retrospectively by Samuel Johnson in his *Lives of the Poets*, were characterized by colloquial diction, abrupt openings, complex theological-scientific analogies, and intellectual wit. Samuel Johnson remarked that in their work 'the most heterogenous ideas are yoked by violence together'. 

John Donne is the supreme master of the metaphysical conceit—an elaborate, far-fetched comparison that functions not merely as decoration, but as the conceptual engine of the poem. In *A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning*, the souls of parted lovers are famously compared to the two stiff legs of a draughtsman's compass: one fixed in the center while the other leans and roams, ensuring a perfect circle upon reunion. Donne's poems, whether dealing with profane erotic desire (*The Flea*) or spiritual anguish (*Batter my heart, three-person'd God*), synthesize sensual passion and rigorous scholastic argumentation.
